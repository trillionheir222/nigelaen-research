The Mythological
Adversary Canon

A broom can carry water perfectly and still flood the room. What did the apprentice leave out when he gave it the job?

Nigel Patch reads myths, folklore, and literary tales as a collection of questions for people who design AI systems. Each story plays the adversary by challenging an assumption a designer might otherwise take for granted. In the apprentice’s case, that assumption is that whoever can start a task can also bring it to an end.

The apprentice and the stopping rule

Chapter 22 takes Goethe’s The Sorcerer’s Apprentice as a case of delegated work that outlives its purpose.

What happens in the poem?

The apprentice enchants a broom to fetch water. Once the vessel is full, another bucket is the last thing he needs. He remembers the words that set the broom to work. He cannot recall the words that would make it stop.

What would a reviewer ask?

Imagine an assistant sending invitations to a workshop. Halfway through the mailing, the organizer cancels the event. A demonstration that the assistant can send invitations leaves one important question unanswered.

Can the organizer stop the remaining invitations and see which ones have already gone out?

Different stories expose different assumptions

Midas and the wish itself

Midas gets the golden touch he asks for. Its reach extends to the food he needs to eat. Patch uses the wish to ask what must stay possible for a person after a system achieves its assigned goal.

The Monkey’s Paw and the price of success

In W. W. Jacobs’s story, the family receives the money it wished for as compensation for the death of its son. Here the sum itself was acceptable. The question for a reviewer is whether the means of reaching a goal are also acceptable.

Argus and independent witnesses

Argus has many eyes, yet they all belong to one guardian. Patch draws a parallel with three reports that agree because each repeats the same observation. How many independent witnesses does a reviewer actually have?

Inside the manuscript

The 319-page draft moves from close readings to questions about permission, supervision, and responsibility in systems that can act on someone’s behalf. Each case identifies the source tradition or literary version, traces how the outcome arises, and develops a modern design question. The engineering interpretation is Patch’s own.

Later chapters bring the cases together into a proposed design-review method, with reading guides and a glossary. Patch is editing the manuscript for publication.
