Theory of Regulated
Intelligence Flow

An AI system can generate answers faster than anyone can check them. A research group can produce proposals faster than it can evaluate them. How much of that extra capacity actually helps someone reach a sound decision in time?

In Theory of Regulated Intelligence Flow, Patch proposes studying how a system turns what it can perceive, remember, and process into useful action. “Flow” gives the question a unit a reader can check. For an answer service, for example, one measure would be how many answers per hour are independently verified as correct and delivered before their deadlines.

One hour to airtime

Imagine a radio producer with ten factual claims to check before a broadcast. Two researchers are free. For this example, each claim takes ten minutes to check, and either researcher can give the producer a correct verdict.

By habit, the producer sends the whole script to one researcher. Six verdicts come back before airtime. Four claims are still waiting, even though the second researcher was available the entire hour.

Now replay that hour with one change. The producer assigns five claims to each researcher. Both finish in fifty minutes. The presenter can now use or correct all ten claims before going on air.

What regulates the flow?

Attention and memory

An archive helps when someone can retrieve a relevant record while it can still change a decision. Retrieving more material can also use up the time available to evaluate it.

Pressure and routing

The radio example adapts the two-route experiment in chapter 17. It also asks what happens if both routes meet again. If one editor must approve every verdict and can sign off on eight per hour, eight is the most the presenter can receive in an hour, even if the researchers check ten.

Stability and recovery

For a team falling behind, two explanations can look alike. It may need time to clear old work, or something may have changed in how it handles new work. The draft asks what happens after the backlog clears.

How would we know?

The 74-page research draft develops its argument across 19 chapters and three appendices. Its mathematical results depend on the assumptions of each model. The proposed experiments ask which of those models predict what happens in an actual system.

One proposed test keeps the incoming work, review budget, and success criteria fixed while changing how cases are routed. The prediction is made before the trial. Every case counts, including difficult ones deferred to another queue. That lets a reader judge whether more people actually received correct answers in time.
